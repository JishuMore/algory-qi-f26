# Q8. Why R-squared is near zero, and whether the rule can still make money

**Why near zero is the expected result, not a bug.** The features here (past returns, 20-day volatility, distance from the 50-day average, relative volume) are the first things anyone builds. Thousands of funds, banks, market makers and students run this same model on this same SPY series, many with faster data and lower costs. If yesterday's return or the gap to the 50-day average reliably predicted tomorrow's return, those traders would buy (or sell) until the price moved and the pattern was gone. What survives that competition is a signal too small to see against daily noise: SPY's daily standard deviation is about 1%, while any plausible predictable part is a few hundredths of a percent. R-squared is the share of variance explained, so it is roughly the square of that ratio, of the order of 0.001 or less. Out of sample, estimation error alone is larger than that, which is why validation and test R-squared come out slightly negative. The tree models make the point clearest: a fully grown random forest gets a training R-squared near 0.8 by memorising noise, and loses on validation. Tuning drives every model toward its simplest form (shallow trees, few boosting rounds, a ridge penalty large enough to shrink the coefficients almost to zero), which is the model saying the best forecast is close to the long-run average return. A strongly positive out-of-sample R-squared from this setup would more likely point to leakage (a feature using day t+1, or shuffled splits) than to a discovery.

**Can the trading rule still make money? Yes, in principle.** R-squared measures how well the predicted *size* of each return matches the actual return, squared error over every day. The long/cash rule uses only the *sign* of the prediction, and its profit depends on which days it skips. Four points follow.

1. **Sign, not size.** A forecast can be poor at magnitudes, and so score R-squared ≤ 0, and still be right about direction slightly more often than chance. A 51–52% hit rate is invisible in R-squared but compounds over hundreds of trades.
2. **Which days matter most.** R-squared weights every day's squared error equally. The rule's profit is dominated by a few large days. If the model tends to move to cash in high-volatility, below-trend periods, where the worst days cluster, it can avoid a large share of drawdown while being wrong on many quiet days. Cutting maximum drawdown and volatility can raise risk-adjusted return even with a lower raw return.
3. **Small, persistent edges are worth money.** A correlation of 0.03 between forecast and outcome means an R-squared of about 0.001, yet across a few thousand independent bets per year that is what many systematic funds operate on (Grinold's information ratio ≈ IC × √breadth). The catch is that one asset, one bet a day, gives very little breadth.
4. **Positive drift.** SPY rises on average, so a model whose predictions are mostly above zero holds the market most days and earns roughly the market return whatever its R-squared. Here that is not skill: compare against buy and hold, not against zero. A heavily penalised ridge often predicts positive on every day and reproduces buy and hold exactly.

**The honest caveats.** One test period of a few years is a small sample; a single crash avoided or missed can decide the result. Switching in and out has transaction costs, bid–ask spread and taxes, none modelled here, and these can remove a thin edge. Cash does earn interest, which would help the rule slightly. So: a near-zero R-squared does not rule out a profitable rule, because R-squared and trading profit measure different things, but it does mean any profit is small, noisy, and has to be checked against buy and hold, after costs, before anyone believes it.
